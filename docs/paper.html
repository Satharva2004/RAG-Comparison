RAG, Vectorless Retrieval, and GraphRAG: A Comparative Study Across Enterprise Verticals

Vector (Pinecone) vs. Vectorless (BM25) vs. Graph (Neo4j) retrieval, evaluated on finance, marketing, product, and service-support corpora

Abstract. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems for enterprise question answering are commonly built on one of three retrieval paradigms: dense vector search, sparse lexical (vectorless) search, and knowledge-graph traversal. Practitioners lack a systematic, cross-domain comparison of when each paradigm — or a hybrid of them — is preferable. We present an evaluation harness that ingests the same document corpus into a vector index (Pinecone), a lexical index (BM25), and a knowledge graph (Neo4j), and measures retrieval hit rate, LLM-judged answer quality, and latency across four enterprise verticals: finance, marketing, product, and customer service. We find that no single paradigm dominates and that a common assumption — that hybrid retrieval is a safe universal default — does not hold: hybrid wins decisively on finance and service but underperforms plain vector retrieval on marketing, where its two component retrievers disagree, a result we verify is not an artifact of a mid-study configuration change via a controlled, top-k-matched re-analysis. We report full results across all 16 (vertical × approach) conditions, Wilson confidence intervals, an ingestion cost/reliability comparison, and a decision framework for practitioners choosing a retrieval architecture under real operational constraints.

1. Introduction

Enterprise deployments of large language models increasingly rely on retrieval-augmented generation to ground responses in proprietary documents. Three retrieval paradigms dominate practice: dense vector retrieval over embeddings, sparse lexical (vectorless) retrieval such as BM25, and graph-based retrieval that traverses entity relationships extracted from the corpus. A fourth, hybrid, approach fuses vector and lexical signals. Prior comparative work (Section 2) evaluates these paradigms on general long-document benchmarks; it does not address whether the ranking of approaches changes across distinct enterprise domains with different document structure — structured financial filings, unstructured marketing copy, product Q&A, and ticket-style customer support text. This paper reports a controlled, same-corpus comparison across those four domains.

2. Related Work

Edge et al. (Microsoft Research) introduced GraphRAG, constructing an LLM-derived entity-relationship graph with community summarization to answer global, corpus-level queries that plain vector RAG struggles with, at the cost of a substantially more expensive indexing pipeline. Asai et al. proposed Self-RAG, which trains a model to adaptively decide when to retrieve and to self-critique retrieved passages, improving factuality without a fixed retrieval architecture. Closest to this work, an industry analysis (Tailored AI, 2025, "RAG vs GraphRAG: A Performance Analysis") benchmarked RAG against GraphRAG on the NovelQA long-document benchmark and on multi-document summarization (SQuALITY, QMSum, ODSum), stratifying queries by single-hop vs. multi-hop reasoning difficulty and scoring with an LLM-as-judge. It found plain RAG preferable for simple factual queries (68.7% accuracy) and GraphRAG preferable for multi-hop and summarization queries, with a hybrid strategy improving on both by roughly 6.4%. That study used one general-purpose document benchmark; it did not vary the underlying domain. This paper adopts a similar LLM-as-judge methodology but varies the enterprise domain instead of query-hop-depth, and adds a vectorless (BM25) baseline absent from the GraphRAG-focused prior work.

3. Proposed Architecture

Figure 1 shows the end-to-end pipeline. A single normalized document corpus per vertical feeds three independent ingestion paths — dense embedding into a vector index, tokenization into a lexical index, and LLM-driven entity/relation extraction into a knowledge graph — so that all four retrieval strategies (vector, vectorless, hybrid, graph) draw on identical source text. Retrieved passages are passed to a shared generation and judging stage, isolating retrieval quality as the only varying factor between conditions.

4.2 Retrieval formulations

Vector retrieval ranks documents by cosine similarity between the query embedding q and document embedding d (Eq. 1):

sim(q, d) = q · d‖q‖ ‖d‖
(1) Cosine similarity — vector retrieval

Vectorless retrieval uses Okapi BM25 over tokenized text, where f(qi, D) is the term frequency of query term qi in document D, |D| its length, and avgdl the corpus average length (Eq. 2):

BM25(D, Q) = Σi=1n IDF(qi) · f(qi,D)·(k1+1)f(qi,D) + k1(1−b+b·|D|/avgdl)
(2) Okapi BM25 — vectorless retrieval

Hybrid retrieval fuses the two rank lists with Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF), where rankr(d) is document d's rank under retriever r and k=60 (Eq. 3):

RRF(d) = Σr∈{vec,bm25} 1k + rankr(d)
(3) Reciprocal Rank Fusion — hybrid retrieval

Graph retrieval extracts an entity set E(q) from the query and ranks documents by shared-entity overlap with each document's extracted entity set E(d) (Eq. 4):

scoregraph(d, q) = |E(q) ∩ E(d)|
(4) Shared-entity overlap — graph retrieval

All three baseline retrievers achieve a perfect 1.0 retrieval hit rate on finance, reflecting the corpus's short, self-contained context passages (10-K filing excerpts) where both semantic and lexical overlap with the question are high. Hybrid edges out vector and BM25 slightly on judge score (4.90 vs. 4.80 / 4.85) — the two retrievers largely agree here, so RRF fusion reinforces a shared correct answer rather than compromising between disagreeing ones (see Discussion). Graph retrieval underperforms the other three on both hit rate (0.70) and judge score (4.10): financial filing text carries few distinct named entities per passage (mostly the same company/ticker repeated), giving the entity-overlap ranking less signal to discriminate between candidate passages than dense or lexical similarity does.

5.2 Marketing

ApproachRetrieval hit rate95% CIAvg. judge score (1–5)Avg. retrieval latency (s)Avg. generation latency (s)

Marketing inverts the finance pattern. Vector is the best approach (4.20, 60% hit rate); BM25 is markedly weaker (3.90, 30% hit rate) since campaign-brief queries share little exact vocabulary with their own strategy text. Critically, hybrid has the lowest judge score on this vertical (1.95, 25% hit rate) because vector and BM25 frequently disagree on their top candidate here, and RRF's rank-averaging then favors documents that are mediocre in both lists over ones that were strong in just one — see §5.6 for a controlled, top-k-matched analysis showing this holds relative to vector specifically, though not as clearly relative to BM25 once an unmatched top-k confound in the raw numbers is corrected for. Graph is also weak (1.80, 35% hit rate): campaign-brief documents are richer in adjectives and strategy language than in the kind of discrete named entities the extraction step targets.

Product shows the weakest scores of any vertical across the board (judge scores 1.80–2.45, hit rates 20–40%). Service is the most unusual result: retrieval hit rate is only 10–15% across every approach, yet judge scores remain the second-highest of any vertical (3.55–4.25) — hybrid wins outright (4.25). This is not a contradiction: the Bitext customer-support corpus is templated by design (e.g. "I understand you have a question about {{Order Number}}..."), so many non-gold passages are close enough in content that the generated answer is still judged correct even when the literal gold passage was not retrieved (see Discussion). This means hit rate alone would misleadingly suggest all four approaches are failing here, when in practice generation quality holds up well regardless of exactly which similar passage was retrieved.

5.5 Discussion

Two patterns cut across the per-vertical results. First, hybrid fusion is conditional, not universal. It wins on finance (4.90) and service (4.25) but has the lowest judge score of the four on marketing (1.95). The determining factor is whether vector and BM25 agree: on finance both retrievers are anchored to the same distinctive company/ticker terms and surface similar top candidates, so Reciprocal Rank Fusion reinforces a shared correct answer. On marketing, the two retrievers often disagree entirely on which document ranks first, and RRF's reciprocal-rank averaging then rewards documents that are merely decent in both lists over a document that was excellent in only one. Practitioners should treat hybrid as a default only after checking retriever agreement on a domain sample, not assume it unconditionally. §5.6 verifies this is not simply an artifact of a mid-study configuration change.

Second, retrieval hit rate and judge score decouple on templated domains. Service shows 10–15% hit rate alongside 3.55–4.25 judge scores; product, by contrast, shows comparable hit rates (20–40%) but much lower judge scores (1.80–2.45). The difference is document homogeneity: service responses are short, generic, and heavily templated, so a "wrong" retrieved passage is often close enough in content to still support a correct-sounding answer, while product Q&A answers are longer and more case-specific, so a wrong retrieval more directly produces a wrong answer. This means retrieval hit rate should be read alongside judge score, and neither in isolation — a high hit rate does not guarantee a good answer (product still scores low even at 40% hit rate), and a low hit rate does not necessarily mean a bad answer (service scores well despite 10–15% hit rate).

5.6 Error Analysis: Isolating the Hybrid Effect from the Top-k Confound

The production run compared marketing hybrid (top-k=3, run after the mid-study configuration change) against marketing BM25 (top-k=5, run before it) — an unmatched comparison. Re-running vector, BM25, and hybrid retrieval at matched top-k (pure retrieval, no LLM calls, so free to verify) gives a cleaner picture:

top-kVectorBM25Hybrid
3 (matched)40% (8/20)20% (4/20)25% (5/20)
5 (matched)60% (12/20)30% (6/20)40% (8/20)

At matched top-k, hybrid still clearly underperforms vector at both settings — that part of the finding is robust, not a top-k artifact. But hybrid is roughly comparable to BM25 at matched top-k (not worse than both, as the unmatched production numbers suggested). The revised, better-supported claim: RRF fusion can drag hybrid below its strongest individual component when the two disagree, without necessarily falling below its weakest component too.

A concrete, matched-top-k example (marketing-doc-5, "Develop a content calendar for a health and wellness blog"): vector@3 retrieves [marketing-155, marketing-88, marketing-5] — a hit, since marketing-5 is the gold passage. Hybrid@3 retrieves [marketing-155, marketing-88, marketing-121] — the same top two documents, but RRF's rank-averaging swaps the correct third document for marketing-121, which ranked better in BM25's list. This is direct, non-confounded evidence of RRF fusion demoting a correct result in favor of a document that ranked passably in both lists rather than excellently in one.

7. Future Work

A follow-up study should: (1) scale the eval set per vertical using a Wilson-CI-informed sample size target (a hit-rate difference of 15–20 points needs roughly 60–100 questions per vertical to separate at 95% confidence, versus 20 here) rather than fitting to an API quota; (2) use multiple judge models or human raters with position-swapped prompts to quantify and correct for LLM-as-judge bias; (3) hold top-k and context length fixed across every condition to remove the mid-study configuration confound entirely; (4) build the graph corpus with a production-grade pipeline (entity resolution/deduplication, community summarization as in Microsoft's GraphRAG) rather than single-pass extraction, since this study's graph results likely understate the approach's ceiling; and (5) report cost per query in dollar terms (embedding + LLM + vector-DB + graph-DB pricing) alongside accuracy, since §6 shows ingestion cost alone can be the deciding factor independent of accuracy differences.

8. Limitations

This study has several limitations that bound the strength of its conclusions. Sample size: 20 questions per vertical (chosen to fit within a free-tier LLM API's daily token quota, which this study exhausted on the evaluation account twice) supports directional observations but not statistically significant claims; Wilson 95% confidence intervals are reported per condition (§5) and are wide enough that most cross-approach differences are not clearly separated at this sample size. Single, unvalidated judge: answer quality is scored by one LLM-as-judge (gpt-oss-120b) with no human validation and no mitigation for judge position bias, a known confound in LLM-as-judge methodology. Mid-study configuration change: to fit within recurring API rate limits, retrieval top-k was reduced from 5 to 3 and per-call context/answer text was truncated (to 400 and 300 characters respectively) partway through evaluation — finance and marketing's vector/BM25 results were produced before this change, while marketing's hybrid/graph and all of product and service were produced after it. §5.6 shows the core marketing finding survives a matched-top-k re-check, but product's and service's absolute scores were not independently re-verified at top-k=5 and should be read with this in mind. Graph corpus scale: capped at 200 documents per vertical with a single-pass LLM entity-extraction step and no entity resolution/deduplication, likely understating graph RAG's potential relative to a production-grade construction pipeline (e.g. Microsoft GraphRAG's community detection and summarization). Hit-rate metric: computed via substring containment against one gold passage per question, which undercounts correct retrievals when the same fact appears in multiple corpus passages, and (per the Discussion above) can diverge from actual answer usefulness on templated domains. Infrastructure reliability: intermittent Neo4j Aura connection drops and one transient DNS/network outage caused several (vertical, approach) combinations to require a retry; a 30-second per-question timeout was added to prevent indefinite hangs, and any question that timed out was scored as a failure (judge score 0) rather than retried, which may slightly depress graph RAG's reported scores relative to its true capability.

9. Conclusion

Across all four enterprise verticals, no single retrieval paradigm dominates, and the ranking of approaches changes by domain: hybrid wins on finance and service where its component retrievers agree, plain vector retrieval wins on marketing and product where it does not. Graph retrieval, in its current entity-overlap formulation, never wins outright in this study, consistent with its documents lacking the dense, distinctive entity structure the approach depends on and with its markedly higher ingestion cost — by far the most expensive and failure-prone stage in the pipeline (§6). The most actionable finding for practitioners is negative: hybrid should not be assumed to be a safe universal default, since a controlled, top-k-matched comparison (§5.6) confirms it can underperform its strongest individual component when the underlying retrievers disagree (marketing), even though it does not necessarily fall below its weakest component too. A second actionable finding is that retrieval hit rate and end-answer quality can diverge substantially on templated, generic-response domains (service), so evaluation should track both metrics rather than either alone. Given the wide, overlapping 95% confidence intervals at n=20 per vertical (§5), these findings should be read as directional evidence motivating a larger follow-up study (§7), not as statistically confirmed differences.
