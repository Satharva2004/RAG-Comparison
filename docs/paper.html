RAG, Vectorless Retrieval, and GraphRAG: A Comparative Study Across Enterprise Verticals

Vector (Pinecone) vs. Vectorless (BM25) vs. Graph (Neo4j) retrieval, evaluated on finance, marketing, product, and service-support corpora

Abstract. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems for enterprise question answering are commonly built on one of three retrieval paradigms: dense vector search, sparse lexical (vectorless) search, and knowledge-graph traversal. Practitioners lack a systematic, cross-domain comparison of when each paradigm — or a hybrid of them — is preferable. We present an evaluation harness that ingests the same document corpus into a vector index (Pinecone), a lexical index (BM25), and a knowledge graph (Neo4j), and measures retrieval hit rate, LLM-judged answer quality, and latency across four enterprise verticals: finance, marketing, product, and customer service. We report preliminary results and a decision framework for practitioners choosing a retrieval architecture under real operational constraints.

1. Introduction

Enterprise deployments of large language models increasingly rely on retrieval-augmented generation to ground responses in proprietary documents. Three retrieval paradigms dominate practice: dense vector retrieval over embeddings, sparse lexical (vectorless) retrieval such as BM25, and graph-based retrieval that traverses entity relationships extracted from the corpus. A fourth, hybrid, approach fuses vector and lexical signals. Prior comparative work (Section 2) evaluates these paradigms on general long-document benchmarks; it does not address whether the ranking of approaches changes across distinct enterprise domains with different document structure — structured financial filings, unstructured marketing copy, product Q&A, and ticket-style customer support text. This paper reports a controlled, same-corpus comparison across those four domains.

2. Related Work

Edge et al. (Microsoft Research) introduced GraphRAG, constructing an LLM-derived entity-relationship graph with community summarization to answer global, corpus-level queries that plain vector RAG struggles with, at the cost of a substantially more expensive indexing pipeline. Asai et al. proposed Self-RAG, which trains a model to adaptively decide when to retrieve and to self-critique retrieved passages, improving factuality without a fixed retrieval architecture. Closest to this work, an industry analysis (Tailored AI, 2025, "RAG vs GraphRAG: A Performance Analysis") benchmarked RAG against GraphRAG on the NovelQA long-document benchmark and on multi-document summarization (SQuALITY, QMSum, ODSum), stratifying queries by single-hop vs. multi-hop reasoning difficulty and scoring with an LLM-as-judge. It found plain RAG preferable for simple factual queries (68.7% accuracy) and GraphRAG preferable for multi-hop and summarization queries, with a hybrid strategy improving on both by roughly 6.4%. That study used one general-purpose document benchmark; it did not vary the underlying domain. This paper adopts a similar LLM-as-judge methodology but varies the enterprise domain instead of query-hop-depth, and adds a vectorless (BM25) baseline absent from the GraphRAG-focused prior work.

3. Proposed Architecture

Figure 1 shows the end-to-end pipeline. A single normalized document corpus per vertical feeds three independent ingestion paths — dense embedding into a vector index, tokenization into a lexical index, and LLM-driven entity/relation extraction into a knowledge graph — so that all four retrieval strategies (vector, vectorless, hybrid, graph) draw on identical source text. Retrieved passages are passed to a shared generation and judging stage, isolating retrieval quality as the only varying factor between conditions.

5.1 Finance (complete)

All three baseline retrievers achieve a perfect 1.0 retrieval hit rate on finance, reflecting the corpus's short, self-contained context passages (10-K filing excerpts) where both semantic and lexical overlap with the question are high. Hybrid edges out vector and BM25 slightly on judge score (4.90 vs. 4.80 / 4.85). Graph retrieval underperforms the other three on both hit rate (0.70) and judge score (4.10): financial filing text carries few distinct named entities per passage (mostly the same company/ticker repeated), giving the entity-overlap ranking less signal to discriminate between candidate passages than dense or lexical similarity does.

Marketing shows a markedly different pattern from finance: BM25's hit rate drops to 0.30 (vs. 1.0 on finance) while vector retrieval holds up better (0.60). Marketing corpus documents are free-form campaign strategy text with high lexical variability and paraphrase-heavy queries (the eval questions are campaign briefs, not literal excerpts of the answer text), which disadvantages exact/near-exact lexical matching relative to dense semantic similarity. This is consistent with the general expectation that vectorless retrieval favors terminology-stable domains (finance, structured tickets) and struggles on paraphrased, free-text domains (marketing).

6. Limitations

This study has several limitations that bound the strength of its conclusions. First, the evaluation set is small — 20 questions per vertical — chosen to fit within free-tier LLM API daily token quotas encountered during this study; it supports directional observations but not statistically significant claims, and confidence intervals are not reported. Second, answer quality is scored by a single LLM-as-judge (gpt-oss-120b) with no human validation and no explicit mitigation for judge position bias, a known confound in LLM-as-judge methodologies. Third, the graph corpus is capped at 200 documents per vertical with a single-pass LLM entity-extraction step and no entity resolution/deduplication, likely understating graph RAG's potential relative to a production-grade knowledge-graph construction pipeline (e.g., Microsoft GraphRAG's community detection and summarization). Fourth, retrieval hit rate is computed via substring containment against a single gold passage per question, which can undercount correct retrievals when the same fact appears in multiple corpus passages. Finally, results for the product and service verticals were incomplete at time of writing due to LLM provider rate limits.

7. Conclusion

Preliminary results across two of four enterprise verticals indicate that no single retrieval paradigm dominates: hybrid (vector + BM25 via RRF) is the strongest or tied-strongest approach where lexical and semantic signals agree (finance), while vector retrieval is more robust than lexical retrieval alone on free-text, paraphrase-heavy domains (marketing). Graph retrieval, in its current entity-overlap formulation, has not yet outperformed the simpler baselines on either completed vertical, suggesting its value may be concentrated in domains with richer, more distinctive entity structure than seen so far (e.g., service tickets linking customers, orders, and products) — to be confirmed once product and service results complete.
